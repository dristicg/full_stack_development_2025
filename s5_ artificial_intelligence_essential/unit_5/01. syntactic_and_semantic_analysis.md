# Syntactic and Semantic Analysis

## 0. Where This Fits

In `00. introduction_to_natural_language_processing.md`, raw text was cleaned, split into tokens, and converted into numbers. That note ended with a warning: **Bag of Words gives `"dog bites man"` and `"man bites dog"` exactly the same vector**, even though the two sentences mean very different things. The numbers lost two things:

- the **structure** of the sentence (which word is doing what, and in what order), and
- the **meaning** of the sentence.

This note brings both back:

- **Syntactic Analysis** recovers the **structure** -- how words group into phrases and how phrases fit together.
- **Semantic Analysis** recovers the **meaning** -- what the words and the whole sentence actually say.

### 0.1 The Levels of Language Analysis

| Level | The Question It Answers | Example |
| :-- | :-- | :-- |
| **Lexical** | What are the words? | Splitting into tokens, reducing `cats` to `cat` (covered in the previous note) |
| **Syntactic** | How are the words arranged? Is the sentence built correctly? | `The cat chased the mouse` = Subject + Verb + Object |
| **Semantic** | What does it mean? | The cat is the one chasing; the mouse is the one being chased |
| **Pragmatic** | What does the speaker intend in this situation? | `Can you pass the salt?` is a request, not a question about your ability |

This note covers the **Syntactic** and **Semantic** levels.

***

## 1. Syntax vs. Semantics -- The Core Difference

### 1.1 Simple Meaning

- **Syntax** is about **form**: the rules for how words may be arranged into a correct sentence (grammar).
- **Semantics** is about **meaning**: what the words and sentences actually say.

### 1.2 Everyday Analogy -- The Proofreader and the Reader

A **proofreader** checks spelling and grammar: *"Is this sentence built correctly?"* A **reader** asks something different: *"Okay, but what is it actually saying, and does it make sense?"* Syntactic analysis is the proofreader. Semantic analysis is the reader.

### 1.3 A Sentence Can Pass One Test and Fail the Other

| Sentence | Syntax Correct? | Semantics Makes Sense? |
| :-- | :-: | :-: |
| `The cat chased the mouse.` | Yes | Yes |
| `Colorless green ideas sleep furiously.` | Yes | **No** -- ideas cannot be colorless and green, and they cannot sleep |
| `Cat the mouse chased the.` | **No** | No -- it is only a jumble of words |
| `Me want food now.` | **No** -- broken grammar | **Yes** -- we still understand what is wanted |

The second sentence is a famous example by the linguist Noam Chomsky. It shows that **perfect grammar does not guarantee meaning**. The fourth shows the reverse: **meaning can survive broken grammar**. This is exactly why NLP needs both kinds of analysis.

***

## 2. Syntactic Analysis

### 2.1 Simple Meaning

**Syntactic Analysis** (also called **parsing**) checks the **grammatical structure** of a sentence: which words belong together as a phrase, and how those phrases are combined. Its usual output is a **parse tree**.

### 2.2 Everyday Analogy -- The Grammar Teacher's Diagram

Remember a school grammar lesson where the teacher took a sentence and drew it on the board: *"This part is the subject, this part is the verb, this part is the object."* Syntactic analysis is that same exercise, performed automatically by a program.

### 2.3 The Running Example and the Four Tools

Every topic in this section uses one sentence:

```
"The cat chased the mouse"
```

```
FROM SENTENCE TO PARSE TREE

   "The cat chased the mouse"
              |
              v
   (1) POS TAGGING     Label every word (noun, verb, ...)
              |
              v
   (2) GRAMMAR RULES   The rulebook of allowed arrangements
              |
              v
   (3) PARSING         Apply the rules to the sentence
              |
              v
   (4) PARSE TREE      The final structure of the sentence
```

***

### 2.4 Topic 1 -- Part-of-Speech (POS) Tagging

#### Simple Meaning

**POS tagging** means **labeling every word with its grammatical role** -- noun, verb, determiner, and so on. It is the very first step of parsing, because the grammar rules work on these labels rather than on individual words.

#### Everyday Analogy

Think of sorting a pile of kitchen items into labeled boxes: knives in one, spoons in another, plates in a third. POS tagging sorts words into labeled boxes (noun, verb, ...) so that later steps know what kind of word they are dealing with.

#### Example

| Word | Tag Used in This Note | Real Tag (Penn Treebank) | What It Means |
| :-- | :-- | :-- | :-- |
| `the` | **Det** (determiner) | `DT` | Points out which noun is meant |
| `cat` | **N** (noun) | `NN` | A thing or animal |
| `chased` | **V** (verb) | `VBD` | An action (past tense) |
| `the` | **Det** | `DT` | Points out which noun is meant |
| `mouse` | **N** | `NN` | A thing or animal |

#### Why Context Matters

The same word can get a **different tag** depending on the sentence:

| Sentence | Word `book` is a... |
| :-- | :-- |
| `I read a book.` | **Noun** |
| `Please book a ticket.` | **Verb** |

So a tagger cannot just look each word up in a dictionary. It has to look at the **neighbouring words** to decide.

***

### 2.5 Topic 2 -- Grammar Rules (Context-Free Grammar)

#### Simple Meaning

A **grammar** is a small **rulebook** that says which arrangements are allowed. Each rule has the form *"this bigger thing can be built from these smaller things."* This kind of rulebook is called a **Context-Free Grammar (CFG)** -- "context-free" because each rule can be applied wherever its left-hand symbol appears, no matter what surrounds it.

#### Everyday Analogy

A LEGO instruction booklet: *"a wall = 4 bricks + 1 window."* Each line tells you how a bigger piece is built from smaller pieces. Grammar rules do the same for sentences.

#### The Symbols

| Symbol | Stands For |
| :-- | :-- |
| **S** | Sentence |
| **NP** | Noun Phrase (a noun with its helpers, such as `the cat`) |
| **VP** | Verb Phrase (a verb with what follows it, such as `chased the mouse`) |
| **Det** | Determiner (`the`, `a`) |
| **N** | Noun |
| **V** | Verb |

Two kinds of symbols appear in rules:

- **Non-terminals** -- labels such as `S`, `NP`, `VP` that can still be **expanded** into smaller pieces.
- **Terminals** -- the **actual words** (`the`, `cat`, ...) where expansion stops.

#### The Grammar for Our Example

| Rule | How to Read It |
| :-- | :-- |
| `S -> NP VP` | A sentence is a noun phrase followed by a verb phrase |
| `NP -> Det N` | A noun phrase is a determiner followed by a noun |
| `VP -> V NP` | A verb phrase is a verb followed by a noun phrase |
| `Det -> the` | The determiner can be the word `the` |
| `N -> cat \| mouse` | A noun can be `cat` or `mouse` |
| `V -> chased` | A verb can be `chased` |

***

### 2.6 Topic 3 -- Parsing and the Parse Tree

#### Simple Meaning

**Parsing** is the process of working out **which grammar rules, applied in which order, produce the sentence**. The result is a **parse tree**:

- the **root** (top) is `S`, the whole sentence,
- every **branch** shows one grammar rule that was used,
- the **leaves** (bottom) are the actual words.

#### Everyday Analogy

A **family tree**. The sentence is the great-grandparent at the top, the phrases are its children, and the individual words are the youngest generation at the bottom. Just as a family tree shows who descends from whom, a parse tree shows which pieces are built from which.

#### How a Parse Tree Is Generated -- Step by Step

The idea is simple: **start with `S`, and keep replacing the left-most unfinished symbol using a grammar rule, until only words remain.** Every time a rule is applied, the symbol on its left becomes a **parent**, and the symbols on its right become its **children** -- that is exactly how the tree grows downward.

```
Step 0  start symbol                S
Step 1  S   -> NP VP                (S NP VP)
Step 2  NP  -> Det N                (S (NP Det N) VP)
Step 3  Det -> the                  (S (NP (Det the) N) VP)
Step 4  N   -> cat                  (S (NP (Det the) (N cat)) VP)
Step 5  VP  -> V NP                 (S (NP (Det the) (N cat)) (VP V NP))
Step 6  V   -> chased               (S (NP (Det the) (N cat)) (VP (V chased) NP))
Step 7  NP  -> Det N                (S (NP (Det the) (N cat)) (VP (V chased) (NP Det N)))
Step 8  Det -> the                  (S (NP (Det the) (N cat)) (VP (V chased) (NP (Det the) N)))
Step 9  N   -> mouse                (S (NP (Det the) (N cat)) (VP (V chased) (NP (Det the) (N mouse))))
```

The brackets on the right are a text way of writing the tree: `(S A B)` means "`S` has two children, `A` and `B`." Reading down the right-hand column, you can watch the tree grow one rule at a time. After Step 9, only words are left, so the tree is complete.

| Step | What Happens |
| :-: | :-- |
| **1** | The whole sentence `S` is split into a subject part (`NP`) and an action part (`VP`) |
| **2-4** | The first `NP` is filled in: `Det` becomes `the`, `N` becomes `cat` |
| **5-6** | The `VP` is split into a verb and an object; the verb becomes `chased` |
| **7-9** | The second `NP` is filled in: `Det` becomes `the`, `N` becomes `mouse` |

#### The Finished Parse Tree

![Parse tree for The cat chased the mouse](https://res.cloudinary.com/deucrairj/image/upload/v1791432930/trtpmeffwuiwsqlqtx5g.jpg)

#### Reading the Tree

| Part of the Tree | Words It Covers | Role in the Sentence |
| :-- | :-- | :-- |
| **S** | the cat chased the mouse | The whole sentence |
| **NP** (left) | the cat | **Subject** -- who does the action |
| **VP** | chased the mouse | **Predicate** -- what is done |
| **V** | chased | The main verb |
| **NP** (right) | the mouse | **Object** -- what the action is done to |

#### The Same Tree, Built Bottom-Up

A parser can also work in the opposite direction -- **from the words upward**:

1. **Tag the words:** `the/Det`, `cat/N`, `chased/V`, `the/Det`, `mouse/N`.
2. Find a rule whose right side matches neighbouring tags: `Det N` matches `the cat`, so build an `NP`.
3. `Det N` also matches `the mouse`, so build a second `NP`.
4. `V NP` matches `chased` + `the mouse`, so build a `VP`.
5. `NP VP` matches `the cat` + `chased the mouse`, so build an `S`.
6. One `S` now covers the whole sentence, so the parse succeeds.

| Direction | Starts From | Idea |
| :-- | :-- | :-- |
| **Top-down** | The symbol `S` | Predict what the sentence must look like, then check it against the words |
| **Bottom-up** | The words and their tags | Combine words into phrases, and phrases into a sentence |

Both directions produce **the same tree**.

#### What Happens When the Sentence Is Wrong?

Take the scrambled sentence `cat the mouse chased the`. Its tags are `N Det N V Det`.

- The grammar says `S` must start with an `NP`.
- An `NP` must start with a `Det`.
- But the first word, `cat`, is a noun.

**No rule can begin a sentence with a noun, so no tree can be built and the parse fails.** A parser is therefore also a **grammar checker**: if no tree exists, the sentence does not fit the grammar. (This tiny grammar is deliberately strict. A real grammar has many more rules -- for example, to allow `Cats chase mice` with no determiner.)

### 2.7 When One Sentence Has Two Parse Trees (Ambiguity)

Consider the sentence:

```
"I saw a man with a telescope"
```

To parse it, the grammar needs a few more rules, including one for a **prepositional phrase (PP)** such as `with a telescope`:

| Rule | How to Read It |
| :-- | :-- |
| `S -> NP VP` | A sentence is a noun phrase followed by a verb phrase |
| `NP -> Pro \| Det N \| NP PP` | A noun phrase can be a pronoun, a determiner plus noun, or a noun phrase followed by a PP |
| `VP -> V NP \| VP PP` | A verb phrase can be a verb plus noun phrase, or a verb phrase followed by a PP |
| `PP -> P NP` | A prepositional phrase is a preposition followed by a noun phrase |

Because a PP can attach to **either** an `NP` **or** a `VP`, this sentence has **two valid parse trees**.

**Tree 1 -- the PP attaches to the verb phrase (`VP`):**

![Parse tree 1 for I saw a man with a telescope](https://res.cloudinary.com/deucrairj/image/upload/v1791432931/eylbc2xkdfwasm5wwoge.jpg)

`with a telescope` describes **how I saw**: **I used a telescope to see the man.**

**Tree 2 -- the PP attaches to the noun phrase (`NP`):**

![Parse tree 2 for I saw a man with a telescope](https://res.cloudinary.com/deucrairj/image/upload/v1791432931/nas8vn0gxeprua0uvlba.jpg)

`with a telescope` describes **the man**: **the man I saw was holding a telescope.**

| | Tree 1 | Tree 2 |
| :-- | :-- | :-- |
| **PP attached to** | `VP` (the action of seeing) | `NP` (the man) |
| **Meaning** | I used a telescope to see him | The man had a telescope |

The words are identical; only the **structure** differs -- and the structure changes the **meaning**. Syntactic analysis can list the possible structures, but it cannot decide which one the speaker meant. That choice needs **meaning and context**, which is where semantic analysis comes in.

***

### 2.8 Another View -- Dependency Parsing

The parse tree above is a **constituency tree**: it groups words into phrases. A **dependency parse** takes a different view: it links words **directly to each other**, showing which word depends on which. For `The cat chased the mouse`, the main verb `chased` is the **root**:

| Head Word | Relation | Dependent Word |
| :-- | :-- | :-- |
| `chased` | `nsubj` (subject) | `cat` |
| `chased` | `obj` (object) | `mouse` |
| `cat` | `det` (determiner) | `The` |
| `mouse` | `det` (determiner) | `the` |

| Type | Focus |
| :-- | :-- |
| **Constituency tree** | Groups words into nested phrases (`NP`, `VP`, ...) |
| **Dependency parse** | Links each word to the word it depends on (subject, object, ...) |

***

## 3. Semantic Analysis

### 3.1 Simple Meaning

**Semantic Analysis** is the process of working out the **meaning** of words and sentences, and checking that the meaning makes sense. Where syntax asks *"is it built correctly?"*, semantics asks *"what does it say?"*

### 3.2 Everyday Analogy

Back to the proofreader and the reader from Section 1. A parse tree is the proofreader's stamp: *"the grammar is fine."* Semantic analysis is the reader asking: *"Good -- but who did what to whom?"*

### 3.3 The Four Topics

| # | Topic | The Question It Answers | Example |
| :-: | :-- | :-- | :-- |
| **1** | **Lexical Semantics** | What do words mean, and how are words related? | `car` means the same as `automobile` |
| **2** | **Word Sense Disambiguation** | Which meaning of this word is intended here? | `bank` (money) or `bank` (river)? |
| **3** | **Semantic Role Labeling** | Who did what to whom, and when? | `Ravi` gave `Priya` a book |
| **4** | **Meaning Representation** | Can the meaning be written in a form a machine can use? | `Give(Ravi, Priya, Book)` |

***

### 3.4 Topic 1 -- Lexical Semantics (Word Meaning and Word Relations)

#### Simple Meaning

**Lexical semantics** studies **what individual words mean** and **how words relate to each other** -- which words mean the same, which are opposites, and which are more general or more specific than others.

#### Everyday Analogy

A **thesaurus and a family tree combined**. A thesaurus tells you that `car` and `automobile` mean the same thing; a family tree tells you that a `sedan` is a kind of `car`, and a `car` is a kind of `vehicle`.

#### Example -- The Word `car`

| Relation | Meaning | Example |
| :-- | :-- | :-- |
| **Synonym** | A word with the same or very similar meaning | `car` = `automobile` |
| **Antonym** | A word with the opposite meaning | `hot` is the opposite of `cold` |
| **Hypernym** | A more general word ("is a kind of" going **up**) | `vehicle` is a hypernym of `car` |
| **Hyponym** | A more specific word ("is a kind of" going **down**) | `sedan` is a hyponym of `car` |
| **Polysemy** | One word with several meanings | `bank` (money) and `bank` (river) |

```
THE WORD HIERARCHY

   vehicle      (more general  = hypernym of car)
      |
     car
      |
    sedan       (more specific = hyponym of car)
```

#### Why It Matters

If someone searches for **"automobile repair"**, a system that knows `automobile` is a synonym of `car` can also return pages that only say **"car repair"**. Without word relations, the search would miss them.

***

### 3.5 Topic 2 -- Word Sense Disambiguation (WSD)

#### Simple Meaning

Many words have **more than one meaning**. **Word Sense Disambiguation** decides **which meaning is intended** in a given sentence, by looking at the **surrounding words**.

#### Everyday Analogy

If a friend says *"I'm going to the bank,"* you know instantly which bank they mean -- from context: they mentioned needing cash, or they were carrying a fishing rod. Your brain uses the surrounding clues. WSD teaches a program to do the same.

#### Example -- The Word `bank`

Suppose the system knows two senses of `bank`, each with a few **clue words** (an illustration -- real systems use dictionaries such as WordNet, or models that read the whole context):

| Sense | Meaning | Clue Words |
| :-- | :-- | :-- |
| **Sense 1** | A place that keeps money | `money`, `deposit`, `loan`, `account`, `cash` |
| **Sense 2** | The edge of a river | `river`, `water`, `shore`, `fish`, `sit` |

**The method, in three steps:**

1. **Clean the sentence** (remove stop words, reduce words to their base form), and set the target word `bank` aside.
2. **Count how many remaining words match each sense's clue words** (the overlap).
3. **Pick the sense with the highest count.**

| Sentence | Context Words After Cleaning | Overlap with Sense 1 (money) | Overlap with Sense 2 (river) | Chosen Sense |
| :-- | :-- | :-: | :-: | :-- |
| `She deposited money in the bank.` | `deposit`, `money` | **2** | 0 | **Sense 1** (money) |
| `He sat on the river bank.` | `sit`, `river` | 0 | **2** | **Sense 2** (river) |

This simple overlap idea is the core of a classic WSD method called the **Lesk algorithm**. The same word `bank` is resolved to two different meanings purely because of the words around it.

***

### 3.6 Topic 3 -- Semantic Role Labeling (SRL)

#### Simple Meaning

**Semantic Role Labeling** finds, for every action in a sentence, **who did it, to what or whom, and when**. It labels each phrase with the **role** it plays in the action -- regardless of how the sentence is worded.

#### Everyday Analogy

A **news reporter's checklist**: *Who? What? To whom? When?* SRL fills in that checklist automatically for each sentence.

#### The Roles Used in This Note

| Role | The Question It Answers | Meaning |
| :-- | :-- | :-- |
| **Agent** | Who did it? | The doer of the action |
| **Theme** | What was acted on or moved? | The thing the action is done to |
| **Recipient** | To whom? | The one who receives |
| **Time** | When? | When the action happened |

(In the widely used PropBank labeling scheme, the roles for `give` are named `Arg0` for the giver, `Arg1` for the thing given, `Arg2` for the receiver, and `ArgM-TMP` for time.)

#### Example -- `Ravi gave Priya a book yesterday.`

| Phrase | Role | Why |
| :-- | :-- | :-- |
| `gave` | **Predicate** (the action) | This is what happened |
| `Ravi` | **Agent** | Ravi is the one who gave |
| `a book` | **Theme** | The book is the thing that was given |
| `Priya` | **Recipient** | Priya is the one who received it |
| `yesterday` | **Time** | It happened yesterday |

#### The Real Power -- Different Wording, Same Roles

Rewrite the sentence in the **passive voice**: `The book was given to Priya by Ravi yesterday.` The grammar changes completely (a different subject, a different word order, a different parse tree), but the **roles do not change**:

| Role | Active: `Ravi gave Priya a book yesterday.` | Passive: `The book was given to Priya by Ravi yesterday.` |
| :-- | :-- | :-- |
| **Predicate** | gave | given |
| **Agent** (who?) | Ravi | Ravi |
| **Theme** (what?) | a book | The book |
| **Recipient** (to whom?) | Priya | Priya |
| **Time** (when?) | yesterday | yesterday |

Notice that in the passive sentence, the **grammatical subject** is `The book`, yet the **Agent** is still `Ravi`. This is the key difference between syntax and semantics: **the grammatical subject is not always the doer.** SRL sees through the grammar to the real meaning.

***

### 3.7 Topic 4 -- Meaning Representation

#### Simple Meaning

**Meaning Representation** writes the meaning of a sentence in a **precise, formal notation** -- usually **logic** -- so that a machine can store it, compare it, and reason with it. This is the same predicate-style notation used in logic: a **predicate** (the action or relation) followed by its **arguments** (the things involved).

#### Everyday Analogy

Turning a sentence into a **spreadsheet row**. The sentence `Ravi gave Priya a book` is free-flowing language; the row `Give | Ravi | Priya | Book` is neat, labeled, and easy for a computer to search.

#### Example -- Built Directly from the Roles in Topic 3

The roles found by SRL slot straight into the notation:

```
Sentence:   Ravi gave Priya a book

Predicate:  Give
Arguments:  Agent = Ravi,  Recipient = Priya,  Theme = Book

Meaning:    Give( Ravi, Priya, Book )
            Order of arguments:  Give( giver, receiver, thing given )
```

#### What the Machine Can Now Do -- Answer Questions

Once the meaning is stored as `Give(Ravi, Priya, Book)`, a question can be answered by **pattern matching**:

| Question | Pattern Searched | Match | Answer |
| :-- | :-- | :-- | :-- |
| `Who gave Priya a book?` | `Give( ?, Priya, Book )` | `Give(Ravi, Priya, Book)` | **Ravi** |
| `What did Ravi give Priya?` | `Give( Ravi, Priya, ? )` | `Give(Ravi, Priya, Book)` | **A book** |
| `Who received the book from Ravi?` | `Give( Ravi, ?, Book )` | `Give(Ravi, Priya, Book)` | **Priya** |

(Sentences with words like *every* use the logical symbols for quantities: `Every student passed` becomes `∀x ( Student(x) → Passed(x) )`, read as "for all x, if x is a student, then x passed.")

***

### 3.8 Same Syntax, Different Meaning

Compare these two sentences:

| Sentence | Parse Tree Shape | Agent (chaser) | Theme (chased) |
| :-- | :-- | :-- | :-- |
| `The cat chased the mouse.` | `(S (NP Det N) (VP V (NP Det N)))` | cat | mouse |
| `The mouse chased the cat.` | `(S (NP Det N) (VP V (NP Det N)))` | mouse | cat |

Both sentences have the **identical parse tree shape** -- the same rules were used, and only the words were swapped. Syntax alone sees **no difference**. Semantics sees that the **roles flipped**, and with them the whole meaning. This is the exact problem from the start of this note (`dog bites man` vs. `man bites dog`), and it is solved only when **both** analyses are used together.

***

## 4. Syntactic vs. Semantic Analysis -- Direct Comparison

| Aspect | **Syntactic Analysis** | **Semantic Analysis** |
| :-- | :-- | :-- |
| **Main Question** | Is the sentence built correctly? How are the words grouped? | What does the sentence mean? Does it make sense? |
| **Focus** | Structure and grammar | Meaning |
| **Typical Output** | Parse tree | Word senses, roles, logical form |
| **Main Tools** | POS tagging, grammar rules, parsers | Word relations, WSD, semantic role labeling |
| **Example Output for `The cat chased the mouse`** | `(S (NP the cat) (VP chased (NP the mouse)))` | `Chase(Cat, Mouse)` with Agent = cat, Theme = mouse |
| **Can Be Fooled By** | Meaningless sentences with good grammar (`Colorless green ideas sleep furiously`) | Sentences with broken grammar that still need decoding (`Me want food now`) |

***

## 5. Putting It Together -- One Sentence, Both Analyses

```
"The cat chased the mouse"
              |
              v   SYNTACTIC ANALYSIS  (structure)
   (S (NP (Det the) (N cat)) (VP (V chased) (NP (Det the) (N mouse))))
              |
              v   SEMANTIC ANALYSIS  (meaning)
   Agent  = cat     (the one doing the chasing)
   Theme  = mouse   (the one being chased)
   Meaning:  Chase( Cat, Mouse )
```

The parse tree answers **"how is the sentence built?"** and the semantic result answers **"what is actually happening?"** Only together do they give a program the full picture that a plain Bag of Words vector loses.

## 6. Summary

| Concept | One-Line Summary |
| :-- | :-- |
| **Syntax** | The rules of how words are arranged into a correct sentence |
| **Semantics** | What words and sentences actually mean |
| **Syntax vs. Semantics** | A sentence can have perfect grammar and no meaning, or broken grammar and clear meaning |
| **POS Tagging** | Labeling every word with its grammatical role; the same word can get different tags in different contexts |
| **Context-Free Grammar** | A rulebook of the form `S -> NP VP` that says how bigger pieces are built from smaller ones |
| **Parsing** | Finding which grammar rules, in which order, produce the sentence |
| **Parse Tree Generation** | Start at `S`, repeatedly replace the left-most unfinished symbol using a rule (parent -> children), until only words remain |
| **Parse Failure** | If no sequence of rules produces the sentence, no tree exists, so the sentence does not fit the grammar |
| **Ambiguity** | One sentence can have several parse trees with different meanings (`I saw a man with a telescope`) |
| **Dependency Parsing** | An alternative view that links each word directly to the word it depends on |
| **Lexical Semantics** | Word meanings and relations: synonym, antonym, hypernym, hyponym, polysemy |
| **Word Sense Disambiguation** | Picking the right meaning of a word using the words around it |
| **Semantic Role Labeling** | Finding who did what, to whom, and when -- the same roles under different wording |
| **Meaning Representation** | Writing meaning as logic (`Give(Ravi, Priya, Book)`) so a machine can reason and answer questions |
| **Why Both Are Needed** | Identical parse-tree shapes can hide opposite meanings (`The cat chased the mouse` vs. `The mouse chased the cat`) |
