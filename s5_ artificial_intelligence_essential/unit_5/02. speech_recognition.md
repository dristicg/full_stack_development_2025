# Introduction to Speech Recognition (ASR)

## 1. What is "Speech Recognition"?

### 1.1 Simple Meaning

**Speech Recognition** (also called **Automatic Speech Recognition**, or **ASR**, and often **speech-to-text**) is a branch of **Artificial Intelligence (AI)** that gives computers the ability to **listen to human speech and write down the words that were spoken** -- in languages such as English or Hindi.

A computer, at its core, only understands **numbers**. Speech, however, is a **vibration of air** -- smooth, continuous, and a little different every time someone says it. Speech recognition is the bridge that **converts sound into numbers, and then turns those numbers into written text**.

### 1.2 Everyday Analogy

Imagine a careful friend who **writes down the words of a song just by listening**. First, they must **hear the separate sounds**. Next, they **group the sounds into words** they already know. Finally, when two different words sound the same, they **pick the one that makes sense in the sentence**. A speech recognition system does exactly these three jobs, one after the other.

### 1.3 Why Is Speech Hard for Computers?

| Challenge | Example | Why It Is Hard |
| :-- | :-- | :-- |
| **Different Speakers** | A child, an elderly man, and a person with a strong accent all say *"water"* | Pitch, speed, and accent change the sound a lot, even though the word is the same |
| **Background Noise** | Talking near a busy road or a fan | The noise is mixed into the same signal as the voice |
| **Similar Sounds** | *"write"* and *"right"*, or *"to"*, *"too"*, *"two"* | The sound is identical, so the sound alone cannot decide the spelling |
| **No Gaps Between Words** | *"ice cream"* and *"I scream"* | People do not pause between words, so the word boundaries are hidden |
| **Informal and Mixed Speech** | *"gonna"*, *"umm"*, or mixing Hindi and English in one sentence | Real speech has fillers, shortcuts, and switches between languages |
| **Speed Variation** | *"hello"* said quickly and *"heeelllooo"* said slowly | The same word can last a fifth of a second or a full second |

### 1.4 Where Is Speech Recognition Used?

| Application | What Speech Recognition Does |
| :-- | :-- |
| **Voice Assistants** | Hears *"set an alarm for six"* and turns it into text a program can act on |
| **Live Captions and Subtitles** | Writes what people say in videos, meetings, and lectures as text on the screen |
| **Voice Typing (Dictation)** | Types a message or document while you speak |
| **Call-Centre Transcription** | Converts recorded customer calls into searchable text |
| **Voice Control (Cars, Homes)** | Lets people give commands hands-free |
| **Accessibility** | Lets people who are deaf or hard of hearing read speech, and lets people who cannot type write by speaking |

### 1.5 Speech Recognition vs. Related Fields

| Field | Direction | The Question It Answers |
| :-- | :-- | :-- |
| **Speech Recognition (ASR)** | Speech to text | "What words were said?" |
| **Speech Synthesis (Text-to-Speech)** | Text to speech | "How do I say these words aloud?" |
| **Speaker Recognition** | Speech to identity | "Who is speaking?" |
| **Natural Language Processing (NLP)** | Text to meaning | "What do these words mean?" |

> **Note:** Speech recognition and NLP work as a team. ASR turns a voice into **text**; NLP then takes that text and works out what it means (for example, that *"turn on the light"* is a command).

## 2. The Big Picture -- From Sound to Text

Before a model can write any words, the sound must travel through a short **pipeline**. This note walks through each stage in detail.

```
THE SPEECH RECOGNITION PIPELINE

   SPOKEN SOUND (air vibrations made by a human voice)
              |
              v
   [ STAGE 1 ]  AUDIO CAPTURE AND DIGITIZATION
                A microphone and sampling turn the sound wave
                into a long list of numbers
              |
              v
   [ STAGE 2 ]  PRE-PROCESSING AND FRAMING
                Clean the signal, then cut it into tiny
                overlapping slices called FRAMES
              |
              v
   [ STAGE 3 ]  FEATURE EXTRACTION
                Describe each frame by the pitches (frequencies)
                it contains: spectrogram / MFCC
              |
              v
   [ STAGE 4 ]  ACOUSTIC MODEL
                Guess which speech sound (phoneme) each frame holds
              |
              v
   [ STAGE 5 ]  DECODING (LEXICON + LANGUAGE MODEL)
                Join the sounds into real words and choose the
                most sensible sentence
              |
              v
   WRITTEN TEXT (a string that an NLP system can use)
```

| Stage | Input | Output |
| :-- | :-- | :-- |
| **1. Audio Capture** | Sound wave | A long list of numbers (samples) |
| **2. Pre-processing and Framing** | List of samples | Many short, overlapping frames |
| **3. Feature Extraction** | Frames | One small list of numbers (features) per frame |
| **4. Acoustic Model** | Feature lists | Sound probabilities for every frame |
| **5. Decoding** | Sound probabilities | The final text |

> **Note:** Many modern systems (for example **wav2vec 2.0** and **Whisper**) use one large neural network that learns Stages 4 and 5 together: audio features go in and text comes out. Older systems used separate hand-built parts for each job. The five stages above are still the clearest way to understand **what has to happen** inside any speech recognizer.

## 3. Sound and Digital Audio

### 3.1 Simple Meaning

**Sound** is a **vibration of air pressure**. A microphone converts that vibration into a changing electrical signal, and the computer then **measures the signal's height many times every second**, storing each measurement as a number. This process is called **sampling** (or **digitization**).

### 3.2 Everyday Analogy

Think of a **flipbook** or a **film reel**. A movie is not truly continuous motion; it is many still pictures shown one after another, so fast that your eye sees smooth movement. Sampling does the same for sound: it takes many tiny "snapshots" of the wave's height each second. Played back in order, the snapshots describe the original wave.

### 3.3 Key Terms

| Term | Meaning | Example |
| :-- | :-- | :-- |
| **Waveform** | The shape of the sound over time | The wiggly line you see in an audio editor |
| **Amplitude** | The height of the wave -- how **loud** it is | A shout has a bigger amplitude than a whisper |
| **Frequency** | How fast the wave repeats, measured in **Hertz (Hz)** -- how **high** the pitch is | A deep voice vibrates roughly 90 times per second (90 Hz); a higher voice roughly 200 times |
| **Sample** | One measurement of the wave's height | The number `0.31` |
| **Sampling Rate** | How many samples are taken each second | 16,000 Hz means 16,000 numbers per second |
| **Bit Depth** | How precisely each sample is stored | 16-bit means 65,536 possible height levels |

### 3.4 Sampling in a Picture

The picture below shows a smooth, slowly repeating wave. The dots (`.`) are the real wave; the stars (`*`) are the moments when we measure it. The row at the bottom is **the list of numbers the computer actually keeps**. (Here the height is rounded to a whole number from -4 to +4 to keep the picture simple.)

```
height (loudness)    "." = the real, smooth wave    "*" = a sample

 +4 |     .*.                     .*.                     .*.
 +3 |   *.   .*                 *.   .*                 *.   .*
 +2 |  .       .               .       .               .       .
 +1 | .         .             .         .             .         .
  0 |*-----------*-----------*-----------*-----------*-----------*
 -1 |             .         .             .         .
 -2 |              .       .               .       .
 -3 |               *.   .*                 *.   .*
 -4 |                 .*.                     .*.
     +-------------------------------------------------------------
val  0 +3 +4 +3  0 -3 -4 -3  0 +3 +4 +3  0 -3 -4 -3  0 +3 +4 +3  0
```

The computer never sees the smooth wave. It sees only the list of numbers `[0, +3, +4, +3, 0, -3, -4, -3, 0, ...]`.

### 3.5 Choosing a Sampling Rate

A wave can only be captured correctly if we sample **at least twice as fast as its highest pitch** (this rule is called the **Nyquist rule**). So the highest pitch we can capture is **half the sampling rate**.

| Sampling Rate | Typical Use | Highest Pitch Captured |
| :-- | :-- | :-- |
| **8,000 Hz** | Telephone calls | 4,000 Hz |
| **16,000 Hz** | Speech recognition | 8,000 Hz |
| **44,90 Hz** | Music CDs | 22,050 Hz |

Most of the information in speech lies below 8,000 Hz, so **16,000 Hz** is the usual choice for speech recognition. Low rates lose the high-pitched parts of speech: on a telephone line, sounds such as `s` and `f` become hard to tell apart.

### 3.6 How Much Data Is That?

| Recording Length | Samples at 16,000 Hz | Size (16-bit, one channel) |
| :-- | :-- | :-- |
| 1 second | 16,000 numbers | 32,000 bytes (about 32 KB) |
| 0.5 second (one short word) | 8,000 numbers | 16,000 bytes |
| 1 minute | 960,000 numbers | 1,920,000 bytes (about 1.9 MB) |

Even one short word is thousands of numbers. That is a lot to handle at once, which is why the next stage cuts the signal into small pieces.

## 4. Pre-processing and Framing

### 4.1 Simple Meaning

Speech changes every few milliseconds -- a quick burst for `t`, then a long steady vowel, then a hum for `n`. Looking at a whole sentence at once would blur all of this together. So the long list of samples is cut into tiny pieces called **frames**. A frame is usually **25 milliseconds** long, and a new frame starts every **9 milliseconds**, so neighbouring frames **overlap**. Inside one frame the sound is almost steady, which makes it easy to describe.

### 4.2 Everyday Analogy

Imagine reading a very long scroll through a **small sliding magnifying glass**. You can see only a few letters at a time, so you slide the glass forward a little, look again, and repeat. Because each slide is **smaller than the glass**, every letter is seen more than once and none gets cut in half unseen. Framing slides a small window along the sound in exactly the same way.

### 4.3 The Numbers Behind Framing

| Setting | Time | Samples at 16,000 Hz |
| :-- | :-- | :-- |
| **Frame length** | 25 ms | 400 samples |
| **Step (distance between frame starts)** | 9 ms | 160 samples |
| **Overlap between neighbouring frames** | 15 ms (25 - 9) | 240 samples |

How many frames fit into **1 second** (16,000 samples)? A frame must fit completely inside the recording, so:

```
frames = 1 + (16000 - 400) / 160 = 1 + 97.5 -> 1 + 97 = 98 frames
```

(Only whole frames count, so the `.5` is dropped.) One second of speech becomes **98 frames of 400 samples each**.

```
time (ms)  0   9  20  30  40  50  60  70
           |---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
audio      ~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~~
Frame 1    [========]   (0 - 25 ms)
Frame 2        [========]   (9 - 35 ms)
Frame 3            [========]   (20 - 45 ms)
Frame 4                [========]   (30 - 55 ms)
Frame 5                    [========]   (40 - 65 ms)

Each character = 2.5 ms.  Frame length = 9 characters = 25 ms.
Each frame starts 4 characters (9 ms) after the one before it.
```

### 4.4 Common Pre-processing Steps

| # | Step | What It Does | Why It Is Needed |
| :-: | :-- | :-- | :-- |
| **1** | **Normalize Volume** | Scales the signal so the loudest part has a standard level | A quiet speaker and a loud speaker then look alike |
| **2** | **Noise Reduction** | Reduces steady background sounds such as a fan hum | The voice stands out more clearly |
| **3** | **Pre-emphasis** | Slightly boosts high pitches, using `y[t] = x[t] - 0.97 * x[t-1]` | High sounds such as `s` are naturally weak and would otherwise be lost |
| **4** | **Windowing** | Multiplies each frame by a smooth curve (for example the **Hamming window**) that fades the frame's edges to near zero | Cutting a frame out with sharp edges creates false pitches that are not in the real sound |

### 4.5 An Important Warning -- Cleaning Is Not Always Free

Noise reduction can **damage the speech itself**. If it is too aggressive, it can erase quiet consonants such as `f` or `th`, so a word like *"fin"* may be heard as *"in"*. Many modern systems are trained on **noisy recordings on purpose**, so they learn to cope with noise instead of relying on heavy cleaning.

## 5. Feature Extraction

### 5.1 Simple Meaning

A frame is still 400 raw numbers that look different for every voice. What the model really needs is a description of **what the sound is made of**: *which pitches are present, and how strong each one is*. **Feature extraction** turns each frame into a short list of numbers (the **features**) that describes exactly that.

### 5.2 Everyday Analogy

Think of the **equalizer bars** on a music player -- the little bars that jump up and down for bass, middle, and treble. The player does not show every tiny wiggle of the sound; it shows **how much of each pitch is present right now**. Feature extraction gives every frame its own set of equalizer bars.

### 5.3 Common Feature Extraction Steps

| # | Step | What It Does | Output |
| :-: | :-- | :-- | :-- |
| **1** | **Fourier Transform (FFT)** | Splits a frame into the pitches it contains and measures the strength of each | For a 400-sample frame at 16,000 Hz: 201 strengths, from 0 Hz to 8,000 Hz in steps of 40 Hz |
| **2** | **Mel Filterbank** | Groups those strengths into bands that are **narrow at low pitches and wide at high pitches**, like human hearing | Typically 26 to 80 numbers |
| **3** | **Logarithm** | Compresses the strengths, because ears hear loudness on a log-like scale | Log band energies |
| **4** | **DCT (only for MFCC)** | Squeezes the log energies into a few independent numbers | Usually **13 MFCC values** per frame |

**MFCC** stands for **Mel-Frequency Cepstral Coefficients**, the classic feature set for speech. Many modern neural networks skip Step 4 and use the **log-mel** values from Step 3 directly.

### 5.4 Why the "Mel" Scale?

Human ears are much better at telling apart **low** pitches than **high** pitches. The same 90 Hz gap is easy to hear at the bottom and almost unnoticeable at the top:

| Pair of Pitches | Gap | How It Sounds to a Person |
| :-- | :-- | :-- |
| 200 Hz and 300 Hz | 90 Hz | Clearly different |
| 7,200 Hz and 7,300 Hz | 90 Hz | Barely different |

The mel scale copies this behaviour, so the features give **more detail where human hearing does** and less detail where it does not.

### 5.5 The Spectrogram -- Seeing Pitch Over Time

If we put the features of all frames side by side, we get a **spectrogram**: time runs left to right, pitch runs bottom to top, and a stronger character means **more energy** at that pitch and moment.

The spectrogram below is of the **simple test audio used in Section 9** -- two pure tones (400 Hz and 1,000 Hz) and some silence, **not real speech**. Real speech looks like many bright, moving bands instead of two flat lines.

- From **500 ms to 700 ms** it is silent again.
- From **700 ms to 850 ms** the high tone returns, and from **850 ms to 1,000 ms** the low tone returns.

### 5.6 Comparing the Number Forms for One Second of Audio

| Form | Represents | Output Shape for 1 Second |
| :-- | :-- | :-- |
| **Raw Samples** | The wave itself | 1 list of 16,000 numbers |
| **Framed Samples** | The wave cut into overlapping slices | 98 frames, each of 400 numbers (39,200 numbers in total, because frames overlap) |
| **MFCC Features** | The pitch makeup of each frame | 98 frames, each of 13 numbers (1,274 numbers in total) |

The features are **more than 12 times smaller** than the raw samples (16,000 / 1,274 is about 12.6) and describe the sound in a way that is much easier for a model to learn from.

## 6. The Acoustic Model -- From Features to Sounds

### 6.1 Simple Meaning

The **acoustic model** is a trained machine learning model (today, almost always a neural network) that looks at the features of a frame -- and a few frames around it -- and says **how likely each speech sound is**. The smallest sounds that can change a word's meaning are called **phonemes**. For example, *"cat"* and *"bat"* differ in just one phoneme: the first one.

### 6.2 Everyday Analogy

Picture a **bird-watcher who has listened to thousands of recordings**. Give them a short clip and they will say, *"That is probably a sparrow -- I am 80 percent sure."* The acoustic model does the same for speech: it hears one short frame and says, *"That is probably the sound `T`, 70 percent sure."*

### 6.3 Phonemes -- The Building Blocks of Words

English has about **40 phonemes**. This note writes them in **ARPAbet**, a simple phoneme alphabet that uses capital letters.

| Word | Phonemes | Notes |
| :-- | :-- | :-- |
| `cat` | `K AE T` | Three sounds |
| `bat` | `B AE T` | Differs from `cat` only in the first sound |
| `turn` | `T ER N` | Three sounds |
| `on` | `AA N` | Two sounds |
| `the` | `DH AH` | Two sounds |
| `light` | `L AY T` | Three sounds |
| `right` | `R AY T` | Differs from `light` only in the first sound (`R` instead of `L`) |

### 6.4 How the Acoustic Model Learns

This is **supervised learning** (see the Machine Learning note): the model is trained on **many hours of recorded speech together with the correct text**. For every frame, the input is the **feature list** and the target is the **correct sound**. Because the target is one of a fixed set of sounds, this is a **classification** task -- the model picks a class (a phoneme) for each frame.

### 6.5 What the Model Outputs for One Frame

For every frame, the model gives **a probability for each possible sound**; the probabilities add up to 1. These numbers are an illustrative example for a frame in the middle of the sound `T`:

| Sound | Probability |
| :-: | :-: |
| `T` | 0.70 |
| `D` | 0.20 |
| `K` | 0.05 |
| all others together | 0.05 |

The best guess for this frame is `T`, but the model also keeps `D` as a real possibility, because `T` and `D` sound alike. The decoder in Section 7 can use that doubt later.

### 6.6 From Many Frames to a Sequence of Sounds

A sound lasts for **many frames**, so the model repeats itself. To get the real sequence of sounds, we **drop the silence and merge repeats**. Here is the word *"light"*:

```
frame guesses :  L  L  L  AY  AY  AY  AY  AY  T  T
                 |  |  |   |   |   |   |   |  |  |
                 +--+--+   +---+---+---+---+  +--+
                    |             |              |
                    v             v              v
after merging :     L            AY              T
```

> **Note:** Merging repeats has one catch: a word such as *"hello"* really contains two `L` sounds in a row. Real systems solve this with a special **blank** symbol placed between them (a method called **CTC**, *Connectionist Temporal Classification*), so that a *real* double sound is not merged away.

## 7. Decoding -- Lexicon and Language Model

### 7.1 Simple Meaning

The acoustic model gives us **sounds**, not sentences. **Decoding** turns the sounds into the **most likely words**. It uses two helpers:

- A **lexicon** (pronunciation dictionary): lists which sounds spell which word.
- A **language model**: scores how likely a sequence of words is in the language.

The decoder combines the **acoustic score** (how well the words match the sound) with the **language score** (how sensible the words are together) and picks the best sentence.

### 7.2 Everyday Analogy

Go back to the friend writing down a song. They hear something that could be *"ice cream"* or *"I scream"*. The two sound almost the same. But if the line before was *"After dinner, we all had some..."*, the friend does not hesitate: it is **"ice cream"**. The sound could not decide; **the surrounding words did**. That is the language model's job.

### 7.3 The Lexicon

| Sounds | Words in the Lexicon |
| :-- | :-- |
| `T ER N` | `turn` |
| `AA N` | `on` |
| `DH AH` | `the` |
| `L AY T` | `light`, `lite` |
| `R AY T` | `right`, `write`, `rite` |

Notice that `light` and `lite` have **exactly the same sounds**, so no amount of listening can separate them. Only the language model can.

### 7.4 The Language Model

A simple language model is built by **counting how often words follow each other** in a huge amount of text. Here are illustrative values for the words that could come after *"turn on the"*:

| Words So Far | Next Word | How Likely |
| :-- | :-- | :-- |
| `turn on the` | `light` | 0.30 |
| `turn on the` | `right` | 0.02 |
| `turn on the` | `lite` | 0.001 |

### 7.5 Combining the Two Scores

For every candidate sentence, **multiply** the acoustic score by the language score (the numbers below are made up for teaching):

| Candidate Sentence | Acoustic Score | Language Score | Combined |
| :-- | :-: | :-: | :-: |
| `turn on the light` | 0.42 | 0.30 | 0.42 x 0.30 = **0.126** |
| `turn on the lite` | 0.42 | 0.001 | 0.42 x 0.001 = 0.00042 |
| `turn on the right` | 0.16 | 0.02 | 0.16 x 0.02 = 0.0032 |

`light` and `lite` get the **same acoustic score**, because they sound identical. The language model breaks the tie. `right` is a real possibility (the first sound was not perfectly clear), but it scores far below `light`. **Winner: "turn on the light".**

> **Note:** A real sentence has many words and each word has several candidates, so the number of possible sentences is enormous. The decoder therefore keeps only the best few candidates at each step. This method is called **beam search**. Real systems also add **log** scores instead of multiplying small probabilities, to avoid numbers that are too tiny for a computer to store.

## 8. Full Worked Example -- One Spoken Sentence, Start to Finish

**The statement** (spoken aloud, in a recording of 1.5 seconds):

```
"turn on the light"
```

### 8.1 The Journey, Step by Step

```
SPOKEN SENTENCE
   "turn on the light"                                       (a 1.5-second recording)
              |
              v   Step 1: DIGITIZE (16,000 samples per second)
   [ 0.01, 0.04, -0.02, 0.07, ... ]                          (24,000 numbers)
              |
              v   Step 2: FRAME (25 ms long, a new one every 9 ms)
   frame 1, frame 2, frame 3, ... frame 148                  (148 frames of 400 numbers)
              |
              v   Step 3: EXTRACT FEATURES (13 MFCC per frame)
   [[-4.1, 2.3, ...], [-3.9, 2.5, ...], ...]                 (148 x 13 = 1,924 numbers)
              |
              v   Step 4: ACOUSTIC MODEL (best sound for every frame)
   SIL x15, T x7, ER x16, N x9, AA x12, N x8, DH x6,
   AH x7, L x9, AY x20, T x9, SIL x30                        (148 sound labels)
              |
              v   Step 5: MERGE REPEATS AND DROP SILENCE
   T ER N | AA N | DH AH | L AY T                            (9 sounds in 4 groups)
              |
              v   Step 6: LEXICON (sounds -> possible words)
   turn | on | the | light / lite / right
              |
              v   Step 7: LANGUAGE MODEL (choose the best sentence)
   "turn on the light"
```

> **Note:** The sample values and feature values in Steps 1 and 3 are made up to show the idea. The **counts** are exact arithmetic: 1.5 s x 16,000 = 24,000 samples; 1 + (24,000 - 400) / 160 = 148 whole frames (the `.5` is dropped); 148 x 13 = 1,924; and 15 + 7 + 16 + 9 + 12 + 8 + 6 + 7 + 9 + 20 + 9 + 30 = 148.

### 8.2 What Each Step Did

| Step | Action | Why It Was Needed | Result |
| :-: | :-- | :-- | :-- |
| **1** | Digitize | A computer only understands numbers | **24,000 numbers** |
| **2** | Frame | Speech changes quickly, but is steady inside a tiny slice | **148 frames** |
| **3** | Extract features | Describe each frame by its pitches, in far fewer numbers | **148 x 13 = 1,924 numbers** |
| **4** | Acoustic model | Link the numbers to real speech sounds | **148 sound labels** |
| **5** | Merge repeats, drop silence | One sound lasts many frames | **9 sounds in 4 groups** |
| **6** | Lexicon | Turn groups of sounds into real words | **A word (or several candidates) per group** |
| **7** | Language model | `light` and `lite` sound the same; context decides | **`turn on the light`** |

### 8.3 The Final Form

The spoken sentence started as **moving air**. After the pipeline it is:

```
Written text :  turn on the light
```

A plain string, ready for **NLP**: it can now be cleaned, split into tokens, and turned into numbers, exactly as described in the NLP note -- and then a system can work out that the user wants the light switched on.

## 9. Measuring Accuracy -- Word Error Rate

### 9.1 Simple Meaning

The standard score for a speech recognizer is the **Word Error Rate (WER)**. Compare the recognizer's text (the **hypothesis**) with the correct text (the **reference**) and count three kinds of mistakes:

| Mistake | Meaning | Short Name |
| :-- | :-- | :-: |
| **Substitution** | A correct word was replaced by a wrong word | **S** |
| **Deletion** | A correct word is missing | **D** |
| **Insertion** | An extra word appeared that was not spoken | **I** |

```
WER = (S + D + I) / N        where N = number of words in the reference
```

A **lower** WER is better, and `0%` is a perfect transcript.

### 9.2 Everyday Analogy

Think of a teacher **marking a dictation test**. The teacher puts the student's page next to the correct text and counts the wrong words, the missing words, and the extra words. WER is that same mark, turned into a percentage.

### 9.3 Worked Example

The reference is `turn on the light`, so **N = 4**.

| What the Recognizer Wrote | S | D | I | Calculation | WER |
| :-- | :-: | :-: | :-: | :-- | :-: |
| `turn on the light` | 0 | 0 | 0 | 0 / 4 | **0%** |
| `turn on the right` | 1 | 0 | 0 | 1 / 4 | **25%** |
| `turn the light` | 0 | 1 | 0 | 1 / 4 | **25%** |
| `turn on the light please` | 0 | 0 | 1 | 1 / 4 | **25%** |
| `turn off the right` | 2 | 0 | 0 | 2 / 4 | **50%** |

> **Note:** WER can go **above 90%** if the recognizer inserts many extra words. It also treats every word as equally important, so mistaking `a` for `the` costs as much as mistaking `light` for `right`.

## 10. Limitations of Speech Recognition

Even a well-built recognizer still has real weaknesses:

| Limitation | Explanation | Example |
| :-- | :-- | :-- |
| **Accents and Dialects** | A model works best on voices like those it was trained on | A system trained mostly on one accent makes more mistakes on another |
| **Noise and Overlapping Speech** | Several sounds are mixed into one signal | Two people talking at the same time, or a loud street |
| **Rare Words and Names** | A word that is missing from the dictionary and the training data is easy to get wrong | A new product name or an unusual person's name |
| **Homophones Need Context** | The sound alone cannot choose between words that sound the same | `write` and `right`; `ice cream` and `I scream` |
| **Mixed and Low-Resource Languages** | Little recorded data exists for some languages, and switching languages mid-sentence is hard | A sentence that mixes Hindi and English, or a language with few hours of recordings |
| **Privacy** | A voice is personal data, and recordings may be stored | A voice assistant that listens for its wake word |

These weaknesses are exactly why modern systems are trained on **huge, varied collections of speech**, why they use **large language models** to repair doubtful words, and why accuracy is always measured on **new, unseen recordings** and not just on the training data.

## 11. Summary

| Concept | One-Line Summary |
| :-- | :-- |
| **Speech Recognition (ASR)** | The branch of AI that listens to speech and writes down the words |
| **Why It Is Hard** | Voices, accents, noise, and look-alike sounds all vary, and speech has no gaps between words |
| **Sampling** | Measuring the sound wave's height many times a second; 16,000 times a second is the usual rate for speech |
| **Frame** | A tiny slice of audio, usually 25 ms long, starting every 9 ms; 1 second gives 98 frames |
| **Pre-processing** | Normalizing volume, reducing noise, pre-emphasis, and windowing |
| **Features (MFCC / log-mel)** | A short list of numbers per frame describing which pitches are present |
| **Spectrogram** | A picture of pitch over time, built from the features of all frames |
| **Phoneme** | The smallest sound that can change a word, such as `L` versus `R` in `light` and `right` |
| **Acoustic Model** | A trained model that gives sound probabilities for every frame |
| **Lexicon** | A dictionary that lists which sounds spell which word |
| **Language Model** | A scorer that says which word sequences are most likely |
| **Decoding** | Combining the acoustic and language scores to pick the best sentence |
| **The Pipeline** | Sound -> Digitize -> Frame -> Features -> Acoustic Model -> Decoding -> Text |
| **Word Error Rate** | (Substitutions + Deletions + Insertions) / words in the reference; lower is better |
| **Key Limitation** | Accents, noise, rare words, and mixed languages still cause mistakes |
